Deep-sea creature
detection & classification

An agentic ensemble pipeline that finds and identifies underwater species. Two YOLOv8 detectors are fused with Weighted Boxes Fusion, then every region is re-checked by a ResNet18 classifier — and the system explains every decision it made. It scores 0.78 mAP in the aquarium it was trained for. The research is about what it does outside that aquarium, and whether it knows when it doesn't know.

Architecture

One image, five stages, three models

A single detector produces confident-looking mistakes. This pipeline makes two independent detectors argue, fuses what they agree on, and then asks a dedicated classifier to confirm the species before anything is shown to you. Each of the four decision points below was built so its effect could be measured rather than assumed — and measuring them is where the research findings come from.

Contribution

Why this isn't just a YOLO wrapper

Wrapping a pretrained detector in an API is a solved problem. The work here is in what happens around the detector — a second opinion on every object, guard conditions on every refinement, and the willingness to return nothing at all. And then the part most pipelines skip: checking whether any of it actually earns its place.

01

Two detectors trained to disagree

The nano and small models differ by far more than capacity. They were trained from different seeds — 42 and 123 — on deliberately different augmentation recipes, with the small model adding mixup at 0.15 and copy-paste at 0.1. Decorrelated failure is the entire point: two models that make the same mistakes tell you nothing by agreeing. WBF then weights them [2, 1] rather than averaging them blindly.

02

A model allowed to say "no"

Two independent checks can stop the run: an unreadable image (blur variance below 5.0), or no single box reaching 0.40. Classification never executes, no boxes are drawn, and the response carries a warning naming the measured value that caused it. Returning nothing is treated as a correct outcome rather than a failure — which matters in underwater imaging, where a confident wrong species is far more damaging than an honest refusal. Keeping the two checks separate matters as much as having them: judging on an average let a crowd of weak detections outvote strong ones, and let an unreadable frame pass whenever the detector was confidently wrong about noise.

03

Refinement that has to prove itself

The zoom-and-recheck pass may only replace a box when the re-detected region overlaps the original at IoU > 0.4 and scores strictly higher. Both guards must hold or the original survives untouched. A second look is permitted to improve an answer, never to invent one — so the refinement step cannot drift onto a different object.

04

Three opinions, not two

When the detector and classifier disagree, the system doesn't pick a favourite. It manufactures a third opinion by re-classifying a looser 15% crop, then takes a majority vote. Two matching opinions win; a genuine three-way deadlock defaults to the detector and forfeits the agreement boost. Either way the disagreement is surfaced in the result rather than quietly resolved.

None of this requires a larger model or more data. It is the same 638-image dataset most aquarium-detection projects use — the difference is entirely in the decision logic wrapped around it, and every one of those decisions is visible in the output.

    Research

    What happens when this system leaves the aquarium

    The metrics below this section are in-domain: bright tanks, seven familiar species. The research question is what happens on real deep-sea footage, where two-thirds of the organisms belong to taxa the model has no label for. Every figure here is measured on dive-disjoint splits with bootstrap confidence intervals — and each one identifies a specific, addressable weakness rather than a general shortfall.

    Why the threshold cannot be tuned to fix it

    Selective risk stays near 0.89 across the whole useful range, and even at the very lowest coverage the floor is about 0.65. The out-of-distribution error rate rises as the confidence bar is raised — the system's most confident deep-sea detections are among its most likely to be confident misidentifications. Confidence carries almost no information about correctness here, so no threshold makes it reliable.

    The finding the numbers point at

    Raw confidence said 0.558 while the system was right 9% of the time. After calibration the score means what it says — though the honest reading is that calibration mostly restores honesty, not skill, since it keeps the ranking a constant predictor could not. The larger result is that the information needed to recognise “this is something new” survives inside the network and is destroyed by the layer that turns it into a species name. A policy trained on the data found this on its own, weighting that novelty signal about five times more heavily than confidence. Recovering signal the architecture was discarding is a design fix, and it is the direction the next phase builds on.

    The result that sets up the next phase

    Each of the four adaptive actions — enhance, zoom, reclassify, re-detect — was forced on every detection and scored against ground truth, on both the frozen and the retrained model. None of them earns its compute. Enhancement does most damage on precisely the images its own trigger selects; zoom changes almost nothing; the majority vote is no better than simply trusting the classifier.

    Nobody had checked. These rules were written on intuition and have been firing on faith since the system was built, and this is the first time any of them has been held to a counterfactual. Removing the layer makes the system more accurate and cheaper — an immediate win — but the durable result is the method: an action that cannot beat not doing it has no business in the pipeline, and the learned policy now has a measured baseline to beat instead of a set of assumptions to imitate.

    Where this goes next

    Three things follow directly. The novelty signal is strongest in the classifier's logits once it is trained on the right domain, so an explicit open-set training objective is the obvious next step rather than a post-hoc score. The learned gate currently decides only accept-or-abstain; extending it to choose which evidence to acquire, under a per-image compute budget, is the remaining contribution. And the same framework carries to video unchanged — a track is just an observation with more evidence attached, which is where the temporal work begins.

    Full numbers, confidence intervals and caveats: experiments/RESULTS.md. Every result here is on a development split; the held-out test set has been read once, to establish the baseline above, and not since — so none of these numbers has been tuned against the data they will finally be judged on.

    Why keep the weaker model?

    The nano detector is not redundant. It was trained with a different seed and a different augmentation recipe, so it fails on different images than the small variant. Weighted Boxes Fusion exploits exactly that — it weights nano at 2 against small at 1 because agreement between two dissimilar models is stronger evidence than a single confident score.
